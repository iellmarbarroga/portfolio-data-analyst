    01 — Overview

    Introduction

    Dengue fever is one of the most pressing public health concerns in tropical countries like the Philippines — a mosquito-borne disease that affects thousands annually. For this project, I explored a five-year dengue case dataset from the Philippine Department of Health and built an interactive Power BI dashboard to surface trends, regional patterns, and seasonal impacts.

    Goal: Give public health decision-makers a clear, interactive view of where dengue hits hardest and when — so interventions can be better targeted.

    02 — Dataset

    Data Overview

    The dataset was sourced from the Philippine Department of Health (DOH) via Kaggle, covering dengue cases and deaths from 2016–2020.

    03 — Preprocessing

    Data Preprocessing

    Before building the dashboard, I cleaned and enriched the raw dataset in Power Query. Three key steps:

    1. Region Standardization

    Region names were inconsistent across rows. I standardized them and mapped each region to its island group (Luzon, Visayas, Mindanao) along with population figures in a separate info_region_clean lookup table — enabling accurate per-capita calculations.

    info_region_clean — island group and population lookup

    2. Seasonal Classification

    To analyze seasonal impact, I converted the text Month column to a month number, then applied a conditional column to label each row as Rainy Season (June–November) or Dry Season (December–May).

    Conditional column logic for rainy vs. dry season classification

    3. Table Relationships

    Finally, I connected the fact and lookup tables in Power BI's Model view via their shared region key, ensuring all cross-table calculations work correctly.

    Table relationships in Power BI Model view

    05 — Analysis & Findings

    Key Findings

    Regional Distribution — Cases

    Luzon recorded the highest case volume at 597,000+ — more than Visayas (280k) and Mindanao (269k) combined. Within Luzon, Region IV-A (CALABARZON), Region III (Central Luzon), and Metro Manila were the top three.

    Total dengue cases by island group and region

    Regional Distribution — Deaths

    While Luzon led in deaths (6.8k), Mindanao was a close second at 5.9k. Notably, NCR (Metro Manila) alone accounted for 4,000 deaths — more than all of Visayas combined.

    Standout Regions

    Region XII (Soccsksargen) had the highest fatality rate at 4.68% despite a relatively low population density of 202/km². Meanwhile, Metro Manila's density of 20,247/km² produced a fatality rate of 3.46% — the second highest — but a surprisingly low prevalence rate of just 0.86%. Region VI (Western Visayas) had the highest prevalence rate of 2.48%.

    Metro Manila vs. outlier regions by fatality and prevalence

    Seasonal Patterns

    Cases surged predictably during the rainy season (June–November), peaking in August–September each year. Dry season months showed significantly lower counts, with only occasional spikes.

    Monthly case distribution — rainy vs. dry season

    06 — Insights

    What the Data Suggests

    High cases ≠ high fatality. Luzon has the most cases but the lowest fatality rate. Mindanao and Visayas have fewer cases but worse outcomes — pointing to healthcare access gaps, not just disease burden.
    Densely populated areas face a different problem. Metro Manila has fewer cases per capita but disproportionately high deaths — likely due to delayed care-seeking in urban congestion, or reporting lags.

    The consistent rainy-season surge (June–November) underscores that preventive resources — vector control, public advisories, hospital capacity — should be pre-positioned by May at the latest, especially in high-risk regions.

    Variations in fatality and prevalence rates across regions argue strongly for differentiated public health responses rather than a one-size-fits-all national policy.

    08 — Conclusion

    Conclusion

    This Power BI dashboard translates five years of raw dengue data into a clear, interactive analytical tool. Beyond basic case counts, it surfaces the quality of health outcomes — fatality rates, prevalence, and regional disparities — that case volumes alone would obscure.

    The inclusion of metrics like fatality rate and prevalence rate makes this a more useful tool for public health officials than a simple case map. Future iterations could incorporate population age structure, hospital bed capacity per region, or weather data to deepen the seasonal analysis.
