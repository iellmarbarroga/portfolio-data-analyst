    01 — Overview

    Introduction

    If you've watched anime from time to time, you might have noticed that there are themes made to target specific demographics. There's anime made for teenage boys and girls, and for adult men and women — or in more accurate terms, shonen, shoujo, seinen, and josei respectively.

    When I started this project, I had one simple question: is there a universally-loved anime across demographics?

    What began as a curiosity turned into a full-scale data analytics project with 500 random users extracted, 984 top-ranked anime, thousands of API calls, and a lot of waiting for rate limits.

    Goal: Use real MAL API data to answer real questions about how people consume anime across age groups and genders — and to find the titles that genuinely transcend demographic boundaries.

    02 — Motivation

    Why This Project?

    I wanted a portfolio project that was technically challenging but also genuinely interesting to me. I'm a long-time anime fan (please don't ask me about my favorite anime or we'd talk for hours — it's Frieren, by the way), and MyAnimeList has one of the richest publicly accessible datasets I could find: user demographics, watching behavior, favorites, scores, genres.

    The goal was simple: use real API data to answer real questions about how people consume anime across age groups and genders.

    03 — Dataset

    The Data

    Everything came from the Jikan REST API v4 — an unofficial but reliable wrapper around MyAnimeList's data. No authentication required, completely free.

    04 — Limitations

    Data Limitations

    Before the findings, here's what you should know about the data:

    On Users

    Only users who filled in their birthday and gender were included. The dataset skews heavily male (73%) which actually mirrors MAL's known gender demographic — 363 Male, 129 Female, 7 Non-Binary. Unfortunately, the Non-Binary count was too small for significant statistical analysis so those entries were dropped.

    On Age Groups

    The 25–34 group makes up 62% of the dataset. They dominate the numbers. The 45+ group only has 16 users so take the findings from it with a grain of salt. In addition, only 1 user under 18 was found so it was dropped.

    User distribution by age group and gender

    On Anime

    The dataset only includes the top 984 anime by score — scores start at 7.9. This is the elite tier of MAL. I originally planned to extract the top 1,000 anime but 26 entries had missing rank values and so these were dropped.

    The first step was extracting just the genre names. ast.literal_eval converts the raw string representation back into an actual Python list, then a list comprehension pulls only the name field from each dictionary. 

    import ast

def extract_genre_names(value):
    try:
        if isinstance(value, str):
            value = ast.literal_eval(value)
        return [item['name'] for item in value]
    except:
        return []

df['genres'] = df['genres'].apply(extract_genre_names)

    06 — Generational Taste

    The Generational Question

    Does your age predict what era of anime you love? Short answer: yes, significantly.

    Audience between 18-24 and 25-34 have their average year of their favorite anime to be Post-2010 while audience between 35-44 and 45+ have their average year of their favorite anime to be Pre-2010.

    Average year of favorite anime by age group

    I ran a one-way ANOVA on the start year of each user's favorite anime grouped by age to determine if there is a significant difference of the average year preferences. F=31.26, p<0.001. Then a Tukey HSD post-hoc to find exactly which groups differed. 

    from scipy import stats
from statsmodels.stats.multicomp import pairwise_tukeyhsd

age_groups = ['18-24', '25-34', '35-44', '45+']

# One-way ANOVA
groups = [df[df['age_group']==g]['fav_year'] for g in age_groups]
f_stat, p_val = stats.f_oneway(*groups)
print(f"F={f_stat:.2f}, p={p_val:.4f}")
if p_value < 0.05:
    print("Result: SIGNIFICANT ✅ — age groups prefer different eras")
else:
    print("Result: NOT SIGNIFICANT ❌ — no era diff between age groups")
    F=31.26, p=0.0000

    Tukey HSD — identifying which age groups significantly differ in anime era preference

    The Tukey HSD post-hoc test revealed where the generational divide actually lies. Three pairs were statistically significant:

    18–24 vs 25–34 (meandiff = −1.22, p = 0.009) — the youngest group favors anime about 1.2 years newer than the 25–34 group. Significant different, but the smallest among all groups. It means that these two groups are broadly watching from the same era.

    18–24 vs 35–44 (meandiff = −5.17, p < 0.001) — the strongest finding. The 18–24 group favors anime roughly 5 years newer than the 35–44 group. These two demographics are watching from almost entirely different eras.

    25–34 vs 35–44 (meandiff = −3.96, p < 0.001) — a clear divide. The 25–34 group also skews toward newer anime compared to 35–44, though not as extreme as the youngest group.

    The 45+ group showed no significant difference against any other age group — not 18–24, not 25–34, not even 35–44. This is almost certainly a sample size problem rather than a genuine finding. With only 16 users in that bracket, the test simply didn't have enough statistical power to detect a difference even if one exists.

    The real generational fault line is the 35–44 group. The era of their favorite anime are significantly different from both younger groups, favoring anime that is 4–5 years older on average compared to what the younger groups' era preference. The 18–24 and 25–34 groups are watching from a much more similar era.

    08 — Gender & Taste

    The Gender Taste Gap

    I ran a chi-square test of independence on genre preference between male and female audiences. χ²=54.10, dof=18, p<0.001. Gender and genre preference are definitively not independent. Genres with fewer than 10 observed entries were excluded from the analysis to satisfy chi-square expected frequency assumptions and ensure reliable results. 

    from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency
contingency_table = pd.crosstab(
  df['genre'], #index
  df['gender'] #columns
)

#Counts below 10 were dropped 
contingency_table = contingency_table[(contingency_table['Female'] > 10) & (contingency_table['Male'] > 10)]

chi2, p_value, dof, expected = chi2_contingency(contingency_table)

print(f"\nChi-square Statistic: {chi2:.4f}")
print(f"P-value: {p_value:.4f}")
print(f"Degrees of Freedom: {dof}")

if p_value < 0.05:
  print("\nResult: SIGNIFICANT ✅ — gender and genre preference are NOT independent")
else:

    from scipy import stats

def is_significant(p):
    print(f"F={f:.4f}, p={p:.4f} — {'SIGNIFICANT ✅' if p < 0.05 else 'NOT SIGNIFICANT ❌'}")

# ── ANOVA: DAYS WATCHED BY AGE GROUP ──────────────
print("ANOVA — Days Watched by Age Group:")
groups = [df[df['age_group'] == ag]['days_watched'].dropna() for ag in age_groups]
f, p = stats.f_oneway(*groups)
is_significant(p)

    10 — Retrospective

    What I'd Do Differently

    Bigger 45+ Sample

    Sixteen users isn't enough to draw strong conclusions about an entire demographic. I'd run the collection longer, specifically targeting older users.

    Non-parametric Tests

    ANOVA assumes roughly normal distribution. Anime watching data is heavily skewed by power users. Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U would have been more appropriate and should be included in a more rigorous version of this analysis.

    11 — Conclusion

    Final Thoughts

    The finding that surprised me most wasn't the gender taste or the generational divide — it was how consistent the 2000s golden era is across demographics. Monster, Full Metal Alchemist, Death Note, Cowboy Bebop, Steins;Gate, One Piece, and Naruto: Shippuuden show up everywhere. Regardless of age, regardless of gender, these anime have become what we call a timeless classic.

    What I didn't expect was how sharply age groups partition by era. The 18–24 and 35–44 groups aren't just slightly different — they're mostly watching from almost entirely separate decades. If I ran this same analysis in 2036, the era distributions would almost certainly shift. The 18–24 group of today will become the 25–34 group of tomorrow — and they'll bring the 2010s with them as their golden era, the same way the current 35–44 group carried the 2000s.

    Whether this generation of anime produces a classic that can survive the test of time with the same universal pull is a question worth revisiting in ten years.
